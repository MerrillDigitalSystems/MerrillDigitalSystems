The core insight

Electrical Search Runs at Two Speeds

And a site that treats them the same converts badly at both.

Look at what people actually type. On one side: "electrician near me," "outlet not working," "breaker keeps tripping," "burning smell from outlet," "half my house has no power." On the other: "panel upgrade cost," "EV charger installation," "generator installation," "electrical inspection," "does a panel upgrade need a permit." Same trade, same phone number, two entirely different human beings.

The first group is in a small emergency. They are standing in a dark hallway holding a phone, they will call one of the first two or three results that looks real, and they are not going to read anything. What they need from your site is a phone number they can tap without scrolling, proof you cover their city, and some signal that a human will answer. If the number is buried below a hero image and three paragraphs about your commitment to quality, they are already calling somebody else.

The second group is the opposite. A panel upgrade, an EV charger, a whole-home generator — these are planned, four-figure decisions that a homeowner researches for days and takes multiple bids on. That person wants a cost range before they talk to anyone, wants to know whether a permit is involved and who pulls it, wants to see photos of finished work, and often wants to know whether you offer financing. They will not call a page that tells them nothing. They will, however, fill out a detailed form on a page that answered three of their questions first.

Almost every electrician website we look at is built as one homepage with one call-to-action for both audiences. The emergency caller scrolls past the mission statement hunting for a number. The researcher hits a contact form with no information behind it and leaves to read someone else's cost guide. Both were leads. Both are gone. The fix is not a redesign — it is building two conversion paths and pointing the right pages at the right one. That distinction is the whole reason this page exists.

The Utah market

What Is Actually Driving Electrical Demand Here

Four things shape what Utah homeowners search for, and each one deserves a page.

EV charger installation is growing fast along the Wasatch Front. Every new EV in a Lehi or Sandy garage eventually becomes a search for a 240-volt circuit, a load calculation, and sometimes a service upgrade to support it. This is one of the newest search categories in the trade, which means the results pages are still thin — often a directory listing, a manufacturer page, and a national installer with no local presence. A genuinely useful page that explains circuit sizing, when the panel has to be upgraded first, what the permit process looks like, and what the job typically runs is one of the more winnable pages an electrician can own right now.

Rooftop solar is common here, and it feeds electrical work. Utah homes take well to solar, and the downstream effect for electricians is steady: main panel upgrades to accommodate a backfeed breaker, service equipment that will not support interconnection, main panel replacements the solar company flagged mid-project, and battery or generator work as a follow-on. Homeowners in that situation are searching in a very specific, anxious way — they were told they need something and want a second opinion on cost. A page written for exactly that moment converts, because almost nobody has written one.

Older Salt Lake City and Ogden housing stock keeps replacement work flowing. The pre-war neighborhoods on the east side of Salt Lake and central Ogden still hold knob-and-tube runs, fuse boxes, ungrounded two-prong outlets, and undersized services never meant to carry a modern kitchen. Those homeowners usually find out during a home inspection or an insurance review, and they search from a position of mild alarm. A calm, plain-English page on fuse box replacement or what knob-and-tube means for a purchase is both a ranking asset and a sales tool.

Utah requires permits and inspections for most electrical work — and homeowners know it. "Do I need a permit for…" is a search a homeowner runs before they call anyone, because they are trying to figure out whether this is a weekend project or a licensed job. Answer it honestly on your site and two things happen: you capture a high-intent search that most competitors ignore, and the reader arrives at your phone number already reassured that you pull the permit and meet the inspector rather than working around it. That is the cheapest trust you will ever buy.

A worked structure

What the Site Map Should Look Like

Structure is the SEO. One page per thing a person searches for.

Google cannot rank a service you have only mentioned in a bullet list on your homepage. Each service that someone searches for by name gets its own page, with its own title, its own explanation, and its own conversion path. For most Utah electrical contractors that means something close to this:

  • ✓Electrical panel upgrades — the anchor page. Cost range, signs you need one, permit and inspection process, timeline.
  • ✓EV charger installation — circuit sizing, load calculation, when a panel upgrade comes first, typical cost.
  • ✓Whole-home generator installation — sizing, transfer switch, gas and permit coordination, winter outage context.
  • ✓Electrical repair & troubleshooting — the symptom page, written in homeowner language, call-first.
  • ✓Lighting, fans & outlets — the smaller recurring work that keeps a route full between big jobs.
  • ✓Commercial & tenant improvement — a completely different buyer, so a completely different page and CTA.
  • ✓Four or five real city pages — the cities you actually drive to, with specifics: housing age, permit office, jobs you have done there.
  • ✓Cost and permit guides — the research content that catches people six weeks before they buy.

Underneath, the technical layer does the connecting: LocalBusiness and Service schema so Google can match your business to the searches, FAQ schema on the pages where you answer real questions, breadcrumbs, a clean sitemap, and Search Console configured so you can actually see which of those pages is earning calls. Skip the twenty-city doorway pages — thin, near-duplicate pages with the city name swapped are a well-known way to hurt a site. Four substantial ones beat twenty hollow ones, every time. Trust for a trade

Electrical Is the Trade Where Proof Matters Most

Nobody worries whether their painter is licensed. They absolutely worry about the person opening the panel.

Put the license number in the footer of every page, not on a hidden "credentials" page. State bonded and insured in plain words. Say explicitly that you pull the permit and meet the inspector — that single sentence removes the objection that stalls more panel upgrades than price does. Put your warranty terms in writing where a homeowner can read them before calling.

Then use real proof. Real reviews with real names, pulled from where they actually live rather than typed into a slider. Real photos of your work — a clean panel you labeled, a charger you mounted, a generator pad you set. Stock photography of someone else's immaculate panel reads as exactly what it is, and homeowners in this trade are unusually good at spotting it. If you have technicians customers ask for by name, name them.
