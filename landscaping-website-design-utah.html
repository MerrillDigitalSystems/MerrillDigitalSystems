The real problem

Almost Nobody Searches for "Landscaping Company"

They search for the one specific thing the calendar just made urgent.

A homeowner in Draper does not wake up wanting a landscaper. They wake up in March to a yard full of dead thatch and search "spring yard cleanup near me." In June a valve fails and they search "sprinkler repair." In October the forecast turns and half the valley searches "sprinkler blowout" inside about three weeks. In December they search "snow removal" — sometimes for the same company, usually without knowing it is the same company.

That is what makes this trade different from every other one we build for. An HVAC company has two predictable peaks and a long shoulder season. A roofer has weather events. Landscaping has a demand curve that rotates through completely different keywords month by month: lawn care near me, sprinkler repair, sprinkler winterization, fall aeration, sod installation, landscape design, retaining wall, snow removal. The volume swing between a term's peak month and its dead month is enormous — and a site built as one static brochure captures a slice of it at best.

The usual landscaping website makes the same mistake: a homepage that says "full-service landscaping," a Services page with a bulleted list, and a gallery. Nothing on that site can rank for "sprinkler blowout Herriman," because nothing on that site is about sprinkler blowouts. The searcher who was ready to book today finds a competitor who gave that job its own page.

The Utah calendar

What Gets Searched, and When

Your site needs a page ready and indexed before each of these arrives — not during.

March–May: The Wake-Up

Spring yard cleanup, dethatching, aeration, sprinkler startup and repair, mulch, and first-cut scheduling. This is the heaviest new-customer window of the year and the one where maintenance contracts are actually won.

June–August: Install Season

Sod installation, landscape design, hardscaping, patios and retaining walls, plus a steady run of sprinkler repair calls as heat exposes every weak valve and broken head in the valley.

October: The Blowout Spike

Sprinkler blowout and winterization is the sharpest, most reliable seasonal search event of the Utah year. The first hard-freeze forecast sends the whole Wasatch Front looking at once, and it is over in weeks.

November–February: Snow

Many Utah crews add snow removal and ice management to keep people employed through winter. That is a real search category with its own commercial contracts — and it needs its own permanent page, not a seasonal banner.

Year-Round: Water-Wise

Xeriscaping, drip conversion, turf removal, and drought-tolerant design. Utah's water situation and its turf-replacement incentive programs have turned this into a genuine search category that most competitors' sites still ignore entirely.

Always On: Maintenance

"Lawn care near me" and "lawn mowing service" never fully disappear, and they carry the highest lifetime value of anything on this list. This is the page that should be selling a season-long plan, not a single visit.

The opening most Utah landscapers miss

Water-Wise Is a Real Search Category Now

And almost nobody in this market has a page for it.

Drought conditions and municipal watering restrictions have changed how Utah homeowners think about a lawn. Turf-removal and water-wise incentive programs have run in parts of the state, HOAs and cities have adjusted their landscape rules, and homeowners who would never have considered replacing grass ten years ago are now searching for xeriscaping, drip irrigation conversion, drought-tolerant plants, and park strip redesign. We are describing a trend, not quoting a rebate figure — programs and amounts change, and your page should point at the current program rather than bake a number into the copy.

Here is why it matters commercially: these are high-ticket design-and-install jobs, and the search results for them in most Utah cities are thin. Established landscapers still lead with green lawns and list "xeriscaping" as the eleventh bullet on a services page. A company with an actual water-wise page — what the process looks like, what plants work in this climate, how a park strip conversion goes, what a drip retrofit involves, photos of finished work — has a structural advantage that is difficult to compete with by adding a bullet point.

It also changes who calls you. Water-wise searchers tend to be planning rather than reacting, which means longer sales cycles, larger tickets, and far less price shopping than a mowing quote.

The seasonal site problem

How to Build a Site Whose Demand Rotates

Three rules. They sound obvious and almost every landscaping site breaks all three.

1. Service pages are permanent. Emphasis is seasonal. Every service you sell gets its own page with its own URL, and that page exists all year whether the phone is ringing for it or not. What changes is what the homepage leads with, which services get featured cards, and what the primary call to action says. In September the hero is about scheduling a blowout; in March it is about cleanup and getting on the mowing route.

2. Deleting the snow removal page in April is the expensive mistake. We see it constantly — a service goes quiet, someone tidies the site, the page comes down. That page had accumulated eight months of indexing, internal links, and whatever authority it earned. In November you republish at a new URL and start from zero, in the one month you needed it. Ranking history is an asset that compounds only if the URL survives the off-season.

3. Publish ahead of the peak, not into it. Indexing and ranking take time. A sprinkler blowout page that goes live in early October is competing against pages Google has known about since July. The seasonal content calendar should run roughly a quarter ahead of the demand curve, which is exactly the opposite of how most businesses think about it.

Practically, we build the rotation into the site so it is a ten-minute change and not a redesign: the hero, the featured-service block, and the form's default service are swappable without touching code. You flip it four times a year. Everything else keeps earning.

What the site should actually sell

The Contract, Not the Cut

A one-off cleanup is a transaction. A maintenance account is an asset — and most landscaping sites are built to sell the wrong one.

What most sites do

  • ✓"Request a quote" as the only call to action, for every service
  • ✓No mention of recurring plans, frequency options, or what is included
  • ✓Seasonal jobs treated as the product, so every customer starts over next year
  • ✓Nothing that tells a blowout customer you also mow, design, or plow

What we build instead

  • ✓Named maintenance plans with clearly listed inclusions and visit frequency
  • ✓A second call to action on every seasonal page: get on the season-long schedule
  • ✓Cross-links that route a one-off searcher toward the recurring offer
  • ✓Forms that capture property details once, so the follow-up is a real quote

The arithmetic is the whole argument. A single fall cleanup is one invoice. The same household on a season-long maintenance plan, renewing, is worth multiples of it across a few years — and it is the same lead, captured by the same form, routed differently. The most visual trade we build for

Your Portfolio Is the Sales Pitch

Nobody asks to see photos of a repaired furnace. Everyone wants to see the yard.

Landscaping sells on outcome, and the outcome is visible. That makes the gallery the highest-value real estate on the site — but only if it is built as a sales tool rather than a dumping ground. Before-and-after pairs from the same angle do more work than any paragraph of copy, especially for design, hardscaping, sod, and water-wise conversions where the customer is trying to picture their own property.

Structure matters as much as content. We group galleries by service and, where there is enough work, by city — so the Sandy page shows Sandy projects and the retaining wall page shows retaining walls. That turns the portfolio into supporting evidence for the exact page a searcher landed on instead of a scroll they abandon.

The technical side is where photo-heavy landscaping sites usually fall apart. Full-resolution phone images dropped straight into a page can make it slow enough that mobile visitors leave before the first picture appears. We compress and convert images to modern formats, size them correctly for the device, lazy-load anything below the fold, set explicit dimensions so the layout does not jump, and write real descriptive alt text — which serves both accessibility and Google Images, a search surface that matters more in this trade than in any other. Investment

What It Costs

Fixed in writing before the build starts. No hourly billing, no monthly retainer required to keep your rankings.

Landscaping sites run $2,000–$8,000. The lower end is a focused build: homepage, four to six service pages, a gallery, forms, and local SEO. The upper end is the full seasonal structure — eight or more service pages, city pages, a large before-and-after portfolio, branching quote forms, and schema across the whole site.

The right comparison is not against a template subscription. It is against the lifetime value of the accounts the site is supposed to bring in. One recurring maintenance customer who stays a few seasons — mowing, spring cleanup, fall aeration, a blowout, maybe a snow contract — is worth several thousand dollars on its own. A site that reliably adds a handful of those accounts a year has paid for itself in its first season and keeps working for free after that. That is a different calculation from a one-off install job, and it is the one we think landscapers should be running.

No guarantees about specific rankings or lead counts — anyone offering those is selling something. What is fixed is the price, the scope, and the fact that you own the code, the domain, and every account when it ships.
