Search behavior

What Utah Homeowners Actually Type

Almost nobody searches "HVAC contractor." They search the symptom.

The queries that produce booked jobs are things like AC repair near me, furnace not igniting, AC not blowing cold air, emergency heating repair, swamp cooler service, and AC tune up. Those are two distinct kinds of customer wearing the same clothes. The first group has a broken system and a house that is getting less comfortable by the hour. The second group is planning — a tune-up before the season, a quote on a replacement, a maintenance plan.

Emergency intent is the one most HVAC websites handle badly. There is no shortlist, no folder of quotes, no reading of an About page. The person is hot or cold, mildly stressed, holding a phone one-handed. They will give your site somewhere between three and ten seconds to prove three things: you fix the thing that's broken, you come to their city, and somebody will answer. If the page is slow, or the phone number is buried under a hero video, they are already back on the results page calling the next company.

Showing up for those searches at all is a separate job from converting them. Google matches a query to a page, not to a business. A single "Services" page listing eleven things in a bulleted row is a weak match for furnace repair West Jordan. A page whose title, headline, body copy, FAQs, and schema are all about furnace repair, linked from the homepage and pointing at the cities you dispatch to, is a strong one. That is the entire structural argument for building an HVAC site as a set of focused pages instead of one long brochure.

Planned-purchase searches deserve the opposite treatment — depth. Someone pricing a high-efficiency furnace install wants ratings, rebates, financing terms, and a sense of install day, and will happily spend four minutes reading. One page written for both audiences serves neither.

Utah seasonality

Four Seasons, Four Different Websites

Wasatch Front HVAC demand is spiky and predictable. The site should move with it.

Spring — Swamp Cooler Startup

Evaporative coolers are still everywhere along the Wasatch Front, and our dry air is why. Every spring means pads, pumps, water lines, and startups — searched as swamp cooler startup, evaporative cooler service near me, and swamp cooler not blowing cold. It runs alongside the first wave of AC tune up bookings.

Summer — July Emergencies

Salt Lake summers push well past 100°F, and AC failures cluster hard in the worst weeks of July. This is peak emergency intent: highest urgency, highest close rate, lowest patience. A site that is slow on cell data loses these calls to whoever loads first.

Fall — Shutdown and Tune-Up

Swamp coolers get drained, covered, and blown out before the first freeze; furnaces get inspected before anyone needs them. This is the calmest, most plannable season — and the right window to publish and index the furnace content you will need in December.

Winter — The First Cold Snap

Demand does not ramp; it detonates. The first hard freeze produces a wall of furnace not igniting, no heat, and emergency heating repair searches in a 48-hour window, often layered with an inversion week when nobody wants to open a window.

Seasonal Homepage Emphasis

The homepage hero, the top navigation item, and the primary call to action should change with the calendar. In June the hero says emergency AC repair. In October it says furnace tune-up. Same site, swapped emphasis — a fifteen-minute edit that we hand you or handle for you.

Publish Ahead of the Season

New pages take weeks to be crawled, indexed, and trusted. A furnace repair page published during the first cold snap is a full season late. We build the calendar backward so each page is live sixty to ninety days early.

Conversion mechanics

What Turns an Emergency Search Into a Call

Two lists. The first is what the panicked customer needs. The second is what makes a stranger trust a trade.

Urgency, handled properly

  • ✓A tap-to-call button above the fold on mobile — not in a footer, not behind a menu
  • ✓Actual service hours printed on the page, including when after-hours coverage starts
  • ✓Honest after-hours language: "we answer 24/7" or "we return calls by 7am" both convert, but only one of them can be true
  • ✓A written service area listing real cities, so a Tooele homeowner isn't guessing
  • ✓Pages that load in about a second on cell data, because that's the network these searches happen on
  • ✓A short backup form for the people who won't call — name, address, "what's wrong," phone

Trust, for a trade

  • ✓Utah contractor license number, plus bonded and insured, visible on every page
  • ✓Manufacturer certifications and dealer status displayed where a homeowner will recognize them
  • ✓Financing options stated up front — a five-figure replacement is a payment question before it's a brand question
  • ✓Real reviews with real names, pulled from the profiles customers can verify themselves
  • ✓Photos of your trucks, your crew, and your actual installs instead of stock imagery
  • ✓Warranty and guarantee terms in plain English, not a PDF nobody opens
Worked example

The Structure We'd Build for an HVAC Company

A representative build, not an existing client — this is the map we'd start from and adjust to your real service mix.

Service pages, one per money-making job. AC repair, AC installation and replacement, furnace repair, furnace installation, swamp cooler service, and maintenance plans. Six pages, each targeting a distinct query family, each with its own headline, FAQs, and internal links. Repair pages are written short and urgent with the phone number repeated; installation pages are written long, with efficiency, rebates, financing, and what install day involves. Maintenance plans get their own page because it is the page that turns one-time emergency callers into recurring revenue.

City pages, only for cities you truly serve. If you dispatch across the Salt Lake Valley, that's West Jordan, Sandy, Draper, Murray, and West Valley City — each with real content about that area, not a template with the city name swapped in. Thin duplicated city pages are a known liability, so we ship five good ones rather than twenty hollow ones.

Schema, three types doing three jobs. LocalBusiness tells search engines who and where you are, with hours, service area, and phone number matching your Google Business Profile exactly. Service markup on each service page ties a specific offering to your business so a service page can qualify for richer results. FAQPage markup on the questions you already answer twenty times a week can earn expanded space in the results — and, increasingly, gets quoted by AI search answers.

Support content that feeds the service pages. A handful of articles answering what homeowners ask before calling — why a furnace short-cycles, what a swamp cooler blowout costs, whether a 20-year-old AC is worth repairing — each linking to the service page that sells the fix. Fixed price, quoted in writing before anything is built.

Most HVAC websites we build land between $2,000 and $8,000.
