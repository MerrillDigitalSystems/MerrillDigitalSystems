The real problem

A Remodel Is Not a Burst Pipe

Almost every contractor website is built on the emergency-trade model. That model is wrong for you.

When a water heater fails, the homeowner searches, scans three results, and calls whichever one looks credible. The whole decision takes ninety seconds, and a fast site with a big phone number wins it. That is a real playbook — it just isn't yours.

Searches like home remodel contractor, kitchen remodel cost, basement finishing, ADU builder, home addition, and general contractor near me behave nothing like that. The same household visits your site four, five, six times over the course of a month or two. The first visit is somebody skimming photos on a phone at nine at night. The second is the cost page. The third is a spouse on a laptop, reading more carefully and asking harder questions. By the fourth, they're comparing you side by side against two other firms with your project pages open in adjacent tabs.

That changes what a good site even is. No sticky call button fixes a decision that takes six weeks. What wins is depth and evidence — enough real material that a fifth visit still turns up something new, and enough proof that when the list gets cut from four firms to two, you're one of the two. A single-page site with a photo grid and a phone number does not survive that comparison, no matter how nice the photo grid is.

The Utah market

What Homeowners Here Are Actually Searching For

Four demand patterns that shape how a Utah contractor's site should be structured.

Basement Finishing Is a Staple

Unfinished basements are close to standard in Utah housing stock, which makes basement finishing a steady, year-round search category rather than a niche one. It deserves its own service page, its own cost content, and its own slice of your project library — not a bullet on a services list.

ADUs Are a Growing Category

Accessory dwelling units have become a real search category as Wasatch Front municipalities have revisited their rules in recent years. The terms are still less contested than kitchen or bath remodeling. Rules differ by city and change, so the page should explain the process honestly and tell readers to confirm with their own municipality.

Growth Sustains Remodel Demand

Sustained population growth along the Wasatch Front keeps additions and whole-home renovations in demand. Families outgrowing a house but reluctant to move look at adding on instead — which is a very different search, and a very different sales conversation, than a cosmetic refresh.

Permits Are a Search Term

Homeowners genuinely worry about permitting, inspections, and how requirements differ from one city to the next. They search those questions before they search for you. A contractor who explains the process in plain language captures that anxiety as traffic — and as trust.

Portfolio

For a General Contractor, the Project Gallery Is the Product

And the single biggest structural mistake is putting it all on one page.

Most contractor sites have a page called Gallery holding sixty images in a grid. It's one URL, competing for one search term, asking the visitor to do the work of finding a project like theirs. Split that same content into one page per project and both problems disappear at once. Thirty project pages are thirty ranking surfaces, each able to win a long-tail search — a basement finish in your specific city, a 1970s kitchen taken back to studs — that a gallery blob will never touch. And a homeowner comparing three firms lands directly on the project that resembles the one in their head.

A project page that does its job contains more than photos:

  • ✓Project type and city or neighborhood right in the title
  • ✓Scope in plain language — what was actually done, room by room
  • ✓Real timeline, start to closeout, not a marketing estimate
  • ✓Budget band, even if approximate, so visitors can place themselves
  • ✓Before, during, and after photos with captions that explain what changed
  • ✓Materials and finishes, since half your visitors are shopping for ideas
  • ✓What went wrong and how it was handled

That last one is the most persuasive block on a contractor's entire website, and almost nobody writes it. Every firm shows the finished kitchen. Very few explain the joist that had to be sistered once the ceiling came down, what it added to the bill, and how the homeowner found out. A paragraph like that does more for a wavering prospect than every badge in your footer combined, because it is the exact thing they are quietly afraid of.

The trust gap

They're Handing You Six Figures and a House Key

A remodel means strangers in the home for months. The site has to answer that before the call happens.

License, Bonded, Insured — as Text

Put the actual license number on the page as readable text, not baked into a badge image. Homeowners who are being careful look it up, and the ones who look it up are the ones who sign contracts.

A Published Payment Schedule

How much is due at signing, what triggers each draw, and what the final payment is tied to. Publishing it costs you nothing and quietly filters out the people who were going to argue about money in month three.

Lien Waivers, Explained

Nearly every homeowner has heard the story about a subcontractor filing a lien on a job the owner already paid for. Explaining what a lien waiver is and confirming you provide them defuses a fear most competitors never even acknowledge.

References and Real Reviews

Live links to your Google reviews, not screenshots. Reviews that name the project type and the city do double duty, since they reinforce exactly the searches you're trying to win.

Warranty in Writing

What's covered, for how long, and how a callback gets handled. Vague reassurance reads as evasion. A specific, limited, written warranty reads as confidence.

Honest Timeline Communication

Publish realistic ranges and name what moves them — material lead times, inspection scheduling, change orders. Homeowners forgive delays they were warned about far more readily than delays they discovered.

Content strategy

Content That Shortens the Cycle

Every page below does two jobs: it ranks, and it moves someone one step closer to calling.

What most contractor sites publish

  • ✓"Every project is unique — call for a quote"
  • ✓A services list with one sentence per service
  • ✓One gallery page holding every photo
  • ✓An About page about the owner's philosophy
  • ✓A contact form and nothing else to do

What earns the shortlist

  • ✓Real cost ranges by project type, with what moves them
  • ✓A process explainer: consult, design, bid, contract, permits, build, punch list
  • ✓A plain-English page on what a change order is and how yours work
  • ✓Honest answers on how long a remodel actually takes
  • ✓A deep, browsable library of individual project pages

Publishing cost ranges is the one that makes contractors nervous, and it is the one worth doing anyway. It will reduce your total inquiry count. That is the point. The homeowner who was never going to spend that much finds out on your website instead of at their kitchen table, and you keep the afternoon. Site structure

How We'd Lay Your Site Out

Each layer exists for a reason. Nothing here is padding.

Service Pages

Kitchen remodel, bathroom remodel, basement finishing, home additions, ADUs, whole-home renovation. One page each, with its own cost range and its own examples — because each of those is a distinct search with distinct intent, and a combined page ranks for none of them.

Individual Project Pages

The engine of the site. Each one is a ranking surface, a conversion asset, and a piece of proof. They accumulate: a site with a project library gets stronger every quarter you keep adding to it.

City Pages

Only for the cities you actually work, and only with real projects on them. Three specific, well-built city pages beat twenty thin ones — and thin duplicates can hurt you rather than help.

Cost, Process, and FAQ Content

The pages a homeowner reads on visit three. They are also the pages that pull in early-stage searches — the people still deciding whether to remodel at all, months before they choose who does it.

Structured Data

Organization and LocalBusiness markup for the firm, Service markup on each service page, FAQPage on the question blocks, BreadcrumbList sitewide, and image markup on project photos. It maps what you do to where you do it in terms search engines can act on.

Speed and Mobile

Contractor sites are photo-heavy, which is exactly how they end up slow. Images get compressed and sized properly, and the site is built to load fast on a phone at night — which is when most of this browsing actually happens.
